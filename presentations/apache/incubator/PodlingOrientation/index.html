Welcome to the Apache Incubator

An introduction to joining the Apache Software Foundation through the Incubator

Welcome to the Apache Incubator orientation. This session introduces new podlings to the incubation process and what to expect as they become part of the Apache Software Foundation (ASF). The Incubator helps projects learn ASF processes, build sustainable communities, and graduate as Apache Top-Level Projects (TLPs). We’ll cover how projects enter incubation, the roles of mentors and the PPMC, how releases and reporting work, and what graduation looks like.

What Is a Podling?

A podling is a project accepted into the Apache Incubator on its journey to becoming an Apache Top-Level Project (TLP).

Before entering, a proposal is submitted with a Champion, a Sponsor, and at least one ASF Member mentor.

Incubation is not only about technology it’s about learning and practicing The Apache Way.

A “podling” is an incubating project that has been accepted into the ASF. Before acceptance, a proposal is reviewed and sponsored by an existing ASF entity (a Top-Level Project or the Incubator PMC). Each project must have at least one ASF Member mentor. Incubation is primarily a learning process — projects adopt ASF culture, legal compliance, and transparent governance.

Goals of Incubation

  • Adopt ASF values: transparency, consensus, meritocracy, community over code, vendor neutrality.

  • Build a diverse and sustainable community.

  • Demonstrate ASF-compliant releases.

  • Ensure legal and policy compliance.

  • Graduate as an independent Apache project.

Incubation ensures projects adopt ASF values, operate transparently, and build diverse communities. The process culminates in graduation, when the project demonstrates independence and alignment with ASF standards.

First Steps

  • Subscribe to project mailing lists (dev@, commits@, private@).

  • Introduce the community and mentors on dev@.

  • Set up ASF infrastructure (Git, website, issue tracker, lists).

  • Review code import with mentor and IPMC guidance.

  • Begin practicing open, list-based decision making.

The first few weeks establish your foundation. Subscribe to all mailing lists and move project work there — decisions must be archived publicly. Introduce mentors and participants on dev@, then set up ASF infrastructure using standard request processes. Infrastructure remains under ASF/Incubator administration until graduation. When importing code, confirm provenance and licensing with mentors. Operate openly from the start - all decisions should be made and recorded on dev@.

Role of Mentors

  • Guide the podling in applying ASF policies and culture.

  • Help interpret ASF rules and avoid common pitfalls.

  • Review and sign off on reports and releases.

  • Provide continuity between the podling and the IPMC.

Mentors are ASF Members who volunteer to guide your project. They interpret ASF processes, review releases and reports, and provide a bridge to the Incubator PMC (IPMC). Mentors advise but do not lead - their goal is to help you become self-governing. Regular mentor engagement is one of the strongest predictors of success.

Podling Project Management Committee (PPMC)

  • Composed of initial committers, mentors, and voted-in PPMC.

  • Oversees the podling’s growth and governance.

  • Votes on releases and community decisions under IPMC oversight.

  • Uses ASF infrastructure, which remains managed by the Foundation during incubation.

The PPMC manages the podling day-to-day. It includes committers, mentors, and others invited based on merit. The PPMC oversees governance and community health, votes on releases, and ensures transparency. All infrastructure (lists, repos, website) is ASF-managed — podlings don’t host their own systems independently.

The Apache Way in Practice

  • Transparency: discussions and decisions on public lists.

  • Consensus: seek agreement; hold formal votes when needed.

  • Meritocracy: influence earned through contribution.

  • Community over Code: people and collaboration come first.

  • Vendor Neutrality: avoid single-company dominance.

The Apache Way defines how the ASF operates. Transparency — open discussion on public lists. Consensus — decisions through dialogue, not authority. Meritocracy — recognition based on contribution. Community over Code — long-term sustainability depends on people. Vendor neutrality — equal participation across organizations.

Releases

  • Each release involves two votes:

    • 1️⃣ Podling (PPMC) vote on dev@ (72-hour minimum).

    • 2️⃣ IPMC vote on general@incubator.apache.org.

  • Must receive at least 3 +1 votes and more +1s than -1s.

  • A -1 vote is not a veto, but signals a concern to resolve.

  • Include the incubating name and ASF disclaimer.

  • Source releases must be staged under dist/dev/incubator/<podling>.

  • Choose the appropriate disclaimer: standard or work-in-progress.

Releases demonstrate that your project can follow ASF’s legal and procedural standards. Each release requires two voting stages: 1. A PPMC vote on dev@, open for at least 72 hours. 2. A binding IPMC vote on general@ for final approval. A valid IPMC vote must achieve at least three +1 votes and more +1s than -1s. A -1 is not a veto — it highlights an issue to resolve through discussion and revision. Releases must include the "(incubating)" name and an ASF disclaimer. Podlings may use the work-in-progress disclaimer if needed. Only source releases are official ASF releases. They must be staged under dist/dev/incubator/<podling> and promoted only after IPMC approval.

Reporting

  • File monthly reports for the first 3 months, then quarterly.

  • Summarize community growth, releases, and challenges.

  • Include mentor comments where appropriate.

  • Reports are reviewed by the IPMC and aggregated for the ASF Board.

Reports track progress and health. The IPMC reviews podling reports and compiles them into the Foundation’s monthly report to the ASF Board. Regular reporting demonstrates accountability and identifies where help may be needed.

Common Pitfalls

  • Making decisions in private chats or company channels.

  • Delaying the first release too long.

  • Expecting mentors to handle process work.

  • Ignoring reports or feedback.

  • Not taking care of project branding and trademarks.

These are common mistakes in early incubation. All decision-making must occur on public lists. Don’t postpone your first release — it’s your main learning milestone. Mentors guide but don’t manage. Always file reports on time. Remeber to make sure 3rd parties are your branding and tradmarks correctly.

Graduation

Podlings are ready to graduate when they:

  • Have a diverse and active community.

  • Operate independently using ASF practices.

  • Have made multiple compliant releases.

  • No longer rely on mentor oversight.

Graduation requires an IPMC vote and ASF Board approval.

Graduation is based on readiness, not time. The IPMC reviews several indicators: community diversity, multiple compliant releases, independence in governance, and a completed status file. When ready, the IPMC votes to recommend graduation to the ASF Board, which gives final approval.

Legal Essentials

  • Licensing: confirm provenance and correct headers.

  • CLAs: individual or corporate contributor agreements as needed.

  • Trademarks: names and logos must be approved and transferred if applicable.

  • Cryptography: follow ASF export and distribution policies.

  • AI-generated content: follow ASF generative-tooling policy and document provenance.

  • ASF Legal Shield: contributors are protected when following ASF-approved processes.

  • Disclaimers: use the standard or work-in-progress disclaimer as appropriate.

ASF legal policies protect contributors and users. All code must have clear provenance and correct headers. Ensure all contributors have signed CLAs (and companies, CCLAs). Coordinate branding and naming with ASF Brand Management — existing trademarks must be transferred or cleared. Follow cryptography export rules. Document and disclose any AI-generated materials per ASF policy. ASF provides a legal shield to protect contributors who follow approved ASF procedures. Always use the correct disclaimer until graduation.

Working in a Global Community

  • Apache is global — account for time zones, languages, and norms.

  • Be inclusive and avoid culture-specific references.

  • Respect volunteer time and differing availability.

Apache is a worldwide, volunteer-driven community. Use clear, respectful language and be mindful of time zones. Avoid idioms or local references that may exclude others. Remember that contributors volunteer their time — inclusiveness builds resilience and trust.

Key Takeaways

  • Incubation is about community as much as code.

  • The Apache Way guides all decisions.

  • Mentors and the IPMC are here to help.

  • Graduation is the destination — openness is the path.

To summarize: Incubation teaches governance, collaboration, and compliance. The Apache Way provides the framework. Mentors and the IPMC ensure support and accountability. Transparency and inclusion pave the way to graduation.

Getting Help

  • Ask mentors on the dev@ list — they’re your first contact.

  • Use general@incubator.apache.org for Incubator-wide questions.

  • Browse lists.apache.org for archived examples.

  • Check the Incubator Wiki for templates, FAQs, and checklists.

Support is always available. Mentors should be your first stop for questions. If something affects multiple podlings, use general@incubator.apache.org. Review list archives for examples, and use the Incubator Wiki’s templates and checklists to stay aligned with best practices.
