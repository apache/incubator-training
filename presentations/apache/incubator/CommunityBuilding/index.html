Community Building in Practice

Turning Contributors into a Sustainable Apache Community

Welcome everyone. Today’s session is Community Building in Practice,one of the most important parts of The Apache Way. At the ASF, we often say “Community over Code.” That’s not a slogan; it’s a survival strategy. Projects that thrive at Apache don’t just write great code, they build great communities. Our focus today is on how to grow, sustain, and strengthen that community, especially during incubation, where the goal is to teach projects how to self-govern and build an independent, diverse, and welcoming culture.

Community vs. Code

  • Code can be easily rewritten, community cannot.

  • Healthy projects attract contributors even when founders move on.

  • Incubation teaches community-building as much as compliance.

Set expectations early. Projects often join the Incubator thinking success equals code maturity, but that’s only half the story. A technically strong codebase is important, but without a strong community, it will stagnate. Incubation is as much about building relationships and trust as it is about licenses and releases.

Why Community Matters

  • "Community Over Code" is a core ASF principle.

  • The ASF model relies on merit earned through contribution and trust.

  • Projects graduate when they show self-governance and diversity, not just technical strength.

In Apache projects, the community is the project. A strong, healthy community ensures that decisions are transparent, no single entity controls the project, and contributors can come and go without disruption.

Code may bring people together, but culture keeps them there. That’s why The Apache Way emphasizes openness, respect, and diversity of thought. Graduation isn’t about feature completeness, it’s about demonstrating self-governance and diversity.

The Lifecycle of Community Growth

  • Formation: a few founders or sponsors propose the podling.

  • Engagement: first contributors join and begin to build trust.

  • Expansion: roles diversify, committers, reviewers, release managers.

  • Sustainability: the project self-governs with minimal mentor oversight.

Communities evolve just like codebases. At first, a few founders or sponsors propose a podling, enthusiasm is high, but diversity is low. As others join, you move through engagement, expansion, and finally sustainability.

Each stage needs different attention from mentors and PPMCs: help newcomers find a place, encourage shared leadership, and eventually step back. A healthy podling will function smoothly even if mentors take a break, that’s the goal.

From Users to Contributors

  • Document how to contribute (README, CONTRIBUTING, website).

  • Label “good first issues” and mentor newcomers.

  • Welcome all forms of contribution, docs, design, testing, discussion.

  • Encourage and recognize new contributors early, recognition builds belonging.

  • Provide a clear “How to Contribute” section on the website.

Every user is a potential contributor, they just need an invitation and a clear path. Podlings should make contribution easy, visible, and rewarding.

Encourage users to start small: documentation fixes, testing releases, or reporting issues. Make sure the contribution process is well-documented and approachable. Remember, the real metric of success isn’t downloads, it’s new people joining and staying.

Supporting New Contributors

  • Explain the patch workflow, review process, and voting culture.

  • Assign mentors or “buddies” for first-time contributors.

  • Encourage experienced contributors to mentor others in turn.

  • Be patient and supportive, learning The Apache Way takes time.

Once contributors show interest, help them succeed. Explain how patches are reviewed, how votes work, and what the project values most. A well-onboarded contributor quickly becomes a confident participant.

Assign mentors or “buddies” to guide new contributors through their first few PRs or mailing list discussions. Encourage experienced contributors to mentor others in turn, this builds continuity and community depth.

Encouraging Meritocracy and Growth

  • Recognize sustained effort, not status.

  • Nominate new committers and PPMC members openly and transparently.

  • Avoid “in-crowds” or hidden decision-making.

  • Encourage shared ownership and delegate trust, empower others to lead.

  • Pair recognition with empowerment, trust contributors with meaningful work.

Meritocracy is central to The Apache Way, but it’s often misunderstood. At Apache, “meritocracy” means trust built through contribution, not authority by seniority. Merit is demonstrated collaboration, not individual status.

A committer is not a privileged title, it’s a recognition of responsibility. Nominate and vote for new committers or PPMC members openly on the private list. Make sure everyone understands how merit is earned and shared.

Balance recognition of effort with opportunities to grow. Invite contributors to help with releases, votes, or documentation leadership.

Retaining and Motivating Contributors

  • Highlight achievements in release notes or blog posts.

  • Delegate trust early, give contributors visible roles.

  • Involve community members in planning discussions.

  • Celebrate milestones.

  • Recognize ongoing, behind-the-scenes contributions.

Retention keeps a community vibrant. Volunteers stay when they feel trusted, valued, and part of something bigger.

Celebrate progress publicly, not just releases. Highlight invisible work like reviewing patches, moderating lists, and onboarding newcomers. Empower contributors to take ownership, people stay where they make a difference.

Ensuring Diversity and Balance

  • Aim for participation from multiple organizations.

  • Encourage contributors from different time zones and backgrounds.

  • Watch for dominance by a single vendor or employer.

  • Share responsibilities broadly.

Diversity isn’t only about representation, it’s resilience. A project dependent on one company, one country, or one time zone is fragile.

Encourage participation across organizations and geographies. Watch for patterns of vendor dominance or single maintainer risk. Mentors should ensure decisions aren’t concentrated in one group.

Diversity of thought and ownership is what makes projects truly open source. Diversity also protects ASF independence, no single entity should ever control direction or decision-making.

Learning from Other ASF Communities

  • Engage with related ASF projects (e.g., reusing libraries, sharing governance patterns).

  • Ask questions on dev@community.apache.org.

  • Participate in Community Over Code events and other conferances.

  • Observe how other projects handle releases, governance, and mentorship.

  • Learn from others, but adapt, don’t copy.

Podlings don’t need to learn everything alone. The ASF is a federation of communities, and some of the best learning happens between projects.

Encourage podlings to observe and collaborate with other ASF projects: reuse libraries, borrow governance patterns, and exchange experiences.

But there’s also a danger in blindly copying another project’s structure or process. Every community has its own size, culture, and maturity. What works for Apache Airflow or Kafka might not work for a small, early-stage podling. Instead, understand why a practice works and adapt it thoughtfully to your own context.

Communication and Inclusion

  • Default to open mailing lists (dev@ > private channels).

  • Write in clear, simple English.

  • Summarize off-list discussions publicly.

  • Assume good faith and patience with newcomers.

  • Use private@ only for sensitive issues (personnel, security, CoC).

  • Encourage respectful collaboration, follow the ASF Code of Conduct.

Communication is governance at Apache.If it didn’t happen on the list, it didn’t happen.

Encourage all decisions and discussions on public mailing lists, not private chats or Slack. Use clear, respectful English, especially since most contributors are non-native speakers. English is the common working language across ASF projects, it ensures everyone can participate transparently and understand shared decisions.

When sensitive issues arise, use private@ responsibly, for personal or legal topics only, and summarize outcomes back to dev@ when appropriate.

Inclusive communication keeps the project transparent and fair.

Handling Conflict Constructively

  • Address issues early through calm, factual discussion.

  • Separate people from problems.

  • Seek consensus, not victory.

  • Escalate respectfully when necessary.

Conflict is natural and sometimes healthy. The key is to turn disagreement into learning, not division.

Encourage people to address issues calmly and focus on facts. Remind everyone that consensus doesn’t mean unanimous agreement, it means all voices are heard and the outcome is acceptable to all.

Mentors can help mediate when needed. If problems escalate, there’s a clear path, mentors, then IPMC, then ASF leadership.

Recognizing and Preventing Burnout

  • Rotate responsibilities like release management or reporting.

  • Appreciate invisible work, moderation, review, testing.

  • Encourage contributors to take breaks.

  • Avoid over-reliance on any single individual.

Sustainability depends on people, not just processes. Volunteers can burn out easily, especially in small or overworked podlings.

Encourage rotation of roles and make it safe for contributors to step back. Acknowledge and thank people for their time. Communities that normalize rest are stronger in the long term.

Measuring Community Health

  • Number of active committers and contributors.

  • Distribution of commits across individuals and organizations.

  • Mailing list participation and tone.

  • Release cadence and shared responsibility.

Metrics can help mentors and PPMCs assess readiness for graduation, but numbers alone don’t tell the full story.

Look for signals of balance: multiple active committers, steady discussions, timely releases, and a constructive tone. Mentors should focus on participation quality, not volume. Remember that numbers alone can mislead, qualitative context, like tone and responsiveness, matters most.

Example Indicators of Podling Health

  • More than three active committers from at least two organizations.

  • Regular releases managed by the podling, not mentors.

  • Friendly and responsive dev@ interactions.

  • Mentors’ involvement decreases as the podling gains confidence.

Here are some common indicators mentors can look for when evaluating community health. These aren’t hard rules, just useful patterns that show maturity and balance. Combine these quantitative signals with qualitative judgment to understand the full picture.

Linking to the ASF Maturity Model

  • Maturity Model areas: Community, Governance, Licensing, Releases, Independence.

  • Strong communities demonstrate openness and shared responsibility.

  • Use the model as a readiness guide, not a box-ticking exercise.

ASF podlings are evaluated not just on code, but on how they embody The Apache Way. Community strength is central to the ASF Maturity Model, especially the “Community” and “Governance” areas. Use the model as a checklist for growth and self-assessment before graduation.

From Mentorship to Self-Governance

  • Mentors guide, PPMC leads.

  • Independence is earned through demonstrated governance.

  • The project must sustain itself beyond mentorship.

Graduation readiness isn’t just about ticking boxes, it’s about leadership transfer. As podlings mature, mentors gradually hand over responsibility to the PPMC. This shift from guided learning to independent governance is the essence of incubation success.

Empowering the PPMC

  • The PPMC owns day-to-day governance.

  • Mentors guide and advise but don’t direct decisions.

  • Encourage the PPMC to lead votes, reviews, and reports.

  • Gradually shift responsibility as confidence grows.

Mentors model behavior, but the PPMC governs. Mentors should enable, not manage.

Encourage the PPMC to lead votes, manage releases, and handle issues independently. Guide them to balance policy compliance with autonomy. A good sign of maturity is when mentors mostly observe rather than intervene.

Common Pitfalls

  • Reliance on one company for commits or resources.

  • Off-list decision-making.

  • Inactive mentors or PPMC.

  • No committer growth or recognition of new contributors.

Here are common warning signs that indicate community risk: vendor dominance, lack of transparency, or no committer growth.

These aren’t failures, they’re signals for corrective action. Mentors should catch them early, raise them in reports, and guide discussion constructively. If you see these warning signs, address them early in mentor notes or reports. Ask guiding questions rather than issuing directives, help the podling discover the solution itself.

Sustaining Engagement After Graduation

  • Keep adding new committers regularly.

  • Maintain transparency and vendor neutrality.

  • Document lessons learned for future contributors.

Graduation isn’t the finish line, it’s the start of independence. A sustainable community keeps mentoring, adding new committers, and growing.

Continue the same good habits from incubation: open communication, inclusive culture, and shared ownership. Graduation means being ready to live The Apache Way without training wheels.

Key Takeaways

  • Community is the true measure of success.

  • Mentorship leads to self-governance.

  • Diversity and openness ensure resilience.

  • Communication keeps trust alive.

  • The Apache Way sustains projects beyond individuals.

Summarize the heart of the presentation: Community strength, diversity, trust, and independence define long-term success. These are the enduring qualities of every great Apache project.

Conclusion

  • Building community is everyone’s responsibility.

  • Healthy communities embody ASF values every day.

  • The goal of incubation is self-sustaining governance and collaboration.

Sustainable communities are what make The Apache Way work.

Let’s wrap up. Community building is the heart of The Apache Way, an ongoing practice of trust, openness, and collaboration.

Every contributor, mentor, and PPMC member has a role in creating a welcoming environment. When you nurture people as carefully as you build software, your project doesn’t just grow, it thrives.
