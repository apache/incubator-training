== Voting and Consensus in Practice
_Turning Discussion into Transparent Decisions_

[.notes]
--
Introduce this session by explaining that decision-making at the ASF is not about authority or majority rule, it’s about **building consensus**.  
Consensus is the foundation of how the ASF operates, both in top-level projects and in podlings during incubation.  
Formal votes are only one way of confirming that consensus has been reached, they’re not where it starts.  

This presentation explores how discussion, lazy consensus, and formal votes fit together,  
how to handle disagreements constructively, and what healthy decision-making looks like in ASF communities.  
Encourage participants to reflect on how their projects make decisions and where they might improve transparency and inclusion.  
--

== Why Consensus Matters

* Consensus is the foundation of ASF governance.
* Formal votes are just one way of measuring it.
* Discussion and shared understanding build trust and transparency.

[.notes]
--
The ASF model is designed to scale without hierarchy.  
Reaching and documenting consensus allows communities to self-govern effectively.  
Consensus gives contributors a voice, prevents dominance by any one entity, and builds trust in community decisions.  
--

== Principles of Consensus Building

* *Community Over Code:* decisions belong to the community, not individuals or companies.
* *Meritocracy:* every voice matters; influence is earned through constructive contribution.
* *Transparency:* decisions are made publicly on the project mailing list.
* *Respect and Inclusiveness:* dialogue and differing views strengthen decisions.

[.notes]
--
These principles reinforce each other.  
“Community Over Code” emphasizes sustainability, people and culture matter more than technology.  
Meritocracy is not about hierarchy, but about earned trust through contribution.  
Transparency and respect ensure that everyone can understand decisions and feel included in them.  
--

== In Practice

* Start discussions seeking broad agreement not votes.  
* Encourage participation and document differing views.  
* Use a vote when consensus isn’t clear, or when a formal record improves transparency.

[.notes]
--
Votes are the *end* of discussion, not the start.  
Always begin with open dialogue: “What do people think?” rather than “Vote +1 or -1.”  
Summarize differing views and document them so the reasoning behind decisions is clear.  
--

== Forms of Consensus

* Lazy Consensus - default for routine actions  
* Consensus After Discussion — used when objections arise  
* Formal Votes - required for binding ASF decisions

[.notes]
--
Consensus takes different forms depending on how much discussion or formality is required.  
Not every decision needs a formal vote, in fact, most don’t.  
The three main types are lazy consensus, consensus after discussion, and formal votes.  
--

=== Lazy Consensus

* The default ASF decision model.  
* A proposal is posted; if no one objects within 72 hours, it’s approved.  
* Works well for routine or low-risk actions like website or documentation updates.  
* Encourages forward progress without bureaucracy.  

_Example:_  
“I’ll merge this pull request in 72 hours unless anyone objects.”

[.notes]
--
Lazy consensus depends on trust and engagement.  
It works best when contributors actively follow the mailing list and feel welcome to speak up.  
Silence from disengaged participants isn’t consensus, it’s apathy, which mentors should watch for.  
--

=== Consensus After Discussion

* Used when objections arise.  
* Discuss until all significant concerns are addressed.  
* Aim for *rough consensus*, broad agreement, even if not everyone fully agrees.  
* Summarize the final outcome publicly.

[.notes]
--
“Rough consensus” means that strong objections have been resolved, not that everyone must agree.  
It’s about inclusion and progress, not unanimity.  
The key is active listening, understanding and adapting to valid concerns until the community feels comfortable moving forward.  
--

=== Formal Votes

* Used when ASF policy or legal requirements require a clear decision.  
* Common examples:  
** Code releases  
** Adding committers or PPMC members  
** Podling graduation proposals  
* Held on the correct list:  
** `dev@` for community matters  
** `private@` for personnel matters  
* Vote options: +1 approve, 0 abstain, -1 disapprove (with explanation)
* A vote can be withdrawn and restarted if significant issues are found during discussion.

[.notes]
--
Formal votes ensure traceability and legal oversight.  
They are not about control but accountability, they demonstrate that ASF policy has been followed.  
A -1 vote signals a concern to address, not a personal rejection.  
If an issue emerges mid-vote, such as a missing NOTICE file, it’s fine to withdraw and restart the vote after fixing it.  
--

== Release Voting in the Incubator

* Two-stage process:  
** 1. **PPMC vote** on `dev@` to confirm podling consensus.  
** 2. **IPMC vote** on `general@` for ASF-level approval.  
* IPMC votes are binding; at least three +1s required.  
* Mentors ensure both votes are clearly linked and summarized.

[.notes]
--
Release voting is often misunderstood.  
The first vote demonstrates that the podling community agrees the release is ready.  
The second confirms that the ASF, through the IPMC, has verified it meets policy and licensing requirements.  
Mentors should ensure both votes are recorded publicly and linked for traceability.  
--

== Who Can Vote and When

[cols="1,1,1,1",options="header,autowidth"]
|===
| Decision | Who Votes | Binding | Mailing List
| Routine actions | Anyone | No | dev@
| Add committers | PPMC | PPMC | private@
| Add PPMC members | PPMC | PPMC | private@
| Releases | pass:[<nobr>PPMC & IPMC</nobr>] | IPMC | dev@ → general@
| Graduation | pass:[<nobr>PPMC & IPMC</nobr>] | IPMC | general@
|===

[.notes]
--
Binding votes come from those formally responsible, PPMC members for project-level matters, IPMC members for Incubator oversight.  
Non-binding votes still matter, they show engagement and inclusiveness.  
All personnel-related votes must take place on the private@ list to protect individual privacy.  
--

== Best Practices for Healthy Consensus

* Start with discussion, not a vote.  
* Summarize the decision clearly afterward.  
* Encourage participation from new contributors.  
* Welcome different perspectives.  
* Treat -1 votes as opportunities to improve.  
* Avoid a “vote-first” culture.  
* Watch for dominance by one company or mentor.  
* Assume good faith.

[.notes]
--
Healthy consensus is cultural, not procedural.  
Encourage open dialogue and avoid rushing to closure.  
When handled respectfully, disagreement builds stronger outcomes and trust.  
--

== Recording Votes

* Post a clear closing summary:  
** Separate *binding* and *non-binding* votes.  
** Include the outcome (e.g., “Passed with 3 binding +1s, no objections”).  
** Link to the original discussion thread.  
* Only votes on ASF-managed mailing lists are valid.  
* Use `[DISCUSS]`, `[VOTE]`, and `[RESULT]` tags for clarity in archives.

[.notes]
--
Vote summaries are vital for transparency and later review.  
Using consistent tags, `[DISCUSS]`, `[VOTE]`, and `[RESULT]` — makes it easier for anyone to follow the decision trail.  
If major issues arise mid-vote, withdraw it, fix the issue, and restart.  
That demonstrates responsibility and integrity, not failure.  
--

== Consensus vs. Majority Rule

* ASF decisions are based on **consensus**, not simple majority.  
* The goal is broad agreement and resolved objections.  
* Votes confirm consensus, they don’t replace discussion.

[.notes]
--
Consensus ensures everyone understands and supports the decision, even if they weren’t the originator.  
It’s about building shared understanding rather than winning arguments.  
--

== Typical Vote Duration

* Minimum of 72 hours for most votes.  
* Longer for complex or contentious topics.  
* Ensures global participation across time zones.

[.notes]
--
Allowing at least 72 hours accounts for contributors across the globe.  
This ensures inclusiveness and fairness, especially in distributed communities.  
--

== Mentor Role in Consensus Building

* Ensure decisions are made publicly and transparently.  
* Guide podlings on when formal votes are required.  
* Help communities avoid private decision-making.  
* Model respectful, inclusive discussion.  
* Step back as the community learns to self-govern.

[.notes]
--
Mentors should model good practice rather than dominate discussion.  
Ask reflective questions — “Is this documented?” or “Have we waited long enough?”  
The goal is for the podling to grow comfortable leading its own governance.  
--

== Escalation and IPMC Support

* If consensus breaks down or process is unclear, mentors can ask for IPMC guidance on `general@`.  
* The IPMC provides advice and oversight, not control.  
* The goal is to help podlings learn and apply ASF practices confidently.

[.notes]
--
Escalating to the IPMC is part of mentorship, not a failure.  
The IPMC ensures that ASF policies and culture are upheld, while letting the podling take ownership of decisions.  
--

== Common Anti-Patterns

[cols="1,1,1",options="header,autowidth"]
|===
| Pattern | Problem | Better Practice
| Private chat decisions | Excludes community | Move all decisions to `dev@`
| Corporate block votes | Undermines meritocracy | Encourage individual voices
| Silence = agreement | Masks confusion/apathy | Ask for feedback explicitly
| Vote before discussion | Misses perspectives | Start with open dialogue
| Ignoring −1 votes | Breaks trust | Discuss and resolve before proceeding
|===

[.notes]
--
These are warning signs of poor process.  
Mentors should watch for them early and guide podlings back toward open, documented, and respectful governance.  
--

== Consensus and Graduation

* Effective consensus building demonstrates community maturity.  
* Graduation indicators include:  
  - Open, respectful discussions.  
  - Participation from multiple organizations.  
  - PPMC independently handling committer and PPMC additions.  
  - Mailing-list record showing inclusive dialogue.

[.notes]
--
Consensus practice is one of the clearest signs a podling is ready to graduate.  
Graduated projects show sustained, self-governing decision-making without mentor intervention.  
--

== Mentor Takeaways

* Encourage discussion before votes.  
* Ensure decisions are visible and well-documented.  
* Confirm both PPMC and IPMC release votes occur and are linked.  
* Use `[DISCUSS]`, `[VOTE]`, and `[RESULT]` tags for clarity.  
* Model patience and inclusion in every decision.  
* Step back as the podling grows toward independence.

[.notes]
--
Mentors are teachers of culture.  
By reinforcing open dialogue, transparency, and respect, they help podlings mature into strong, self-governing communities ready for graduation.  
--

== Key Takeaway

* Consensus is conversation first, voting second.  
* Healthy communities:  
** Discuss openly  
** Document clearly  
** Include all voices  
** Build trust through transparency  

[.notes]
--
Close by emphasizing that consensus is what sustains Apache projects over time.  
When communities listen, record decisions clearly, and value every perspective, they embody the heart of The Apache Way.  
--
