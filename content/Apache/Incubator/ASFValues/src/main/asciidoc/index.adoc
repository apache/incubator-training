== 🌱 ASF Culture Modules
Learning the Values Behind the Apache Way

[.notes]
--
Welcome participants.  
Explain that incubation isn’t just about technical milestones or ticking boxes.  
It’s about learning and living the cultural values that make Apache projects sustainable.  
These modules are designed to help podlings understand those values and apply them in daily work.  
Encourage participants to see this not as abstract philosophy but as practical skills for building healthy communities.  
--

== Why Culture Matters
- Processes can be copied; culture must be learned  
- Sustaining a project requires more than code  
- ASF values guide how communities make decisions, grow, and resolve conflict  

[.notes]
--
Point out that any group can copy Apache’s mailing lists, repos, and voting rules — but without the cultural understanding, the project may still fail.  
Highlight that long-lived projects survive because they have communities who know how to handle conflict, invite newcomers, and adapt.  
This is why ASF culture is core to incubation, not just governance paperwork.  
--

== What We’ll Cover
Six modules aligned with the typical podling journey

1. Transparency  
2. Consensus Decision-Making  
3. Meritocracy & Community Growth  
4. Community over Code  
5. Vendor Neutrality  
6. Branding & Trademarks  

[.notes]
--
Explain the order: new podlings often struggle first with transparency and consensus, then questions of meritocracy and growth, then deeper challenges like vendor dominance and branding issues.  
This sequence mirrors real podling journeys, but each module also stands alone.  
--

== How to Use These Modules
- Best followed in sequence, but each stands alone  
- Embedded in podling training, checkpoints, and mentor discussions  
- Reflection questions are prompts for mailing list or mentor conversations  
- Quizzes are for self-check, not grading  

[.notes]
--
Stress that this isn’t school — no one is grading answers.  
Reflection questions are designed to spark useful discussions.  
Encourage participants to take examples back to their podling lists, where conversations matter most.  
--

== Reflection Question
Which of these ASF values do you think your podling already practices well, and which might need more work?  

[.notes]
--
This is a warm-up question. Encourage participants to be candid — no podling is perfect.  
Answers can help mentors focus support where it’s most needed.

== 🔍 Transparency
*Decisions in the Open*

[.notes]
--
Introduce transparency as Apache’s foundational cultural value.  
At Apache, important decisions must be visible to the entire community, archived, and open to participation.  
Without transparency, other values like consensus and meritocracy cannot function.  
--

== What Transparency Means
- Technical and governance discussions happen on public lists  
- Private channels (chat, calls, Slack) are fine for brainstorming, but outcomes must be written back to the list  
- Mailing lists are the **source of record** for project decisions  
- “If it didn’t happen on the list, it didn’t happen.”  

== Why Transparency Matters
- Ensures accountability and trust within the community  
- Allows new contributors to follow history and context  
- Prevents “insider” and “outsider” dynamics  
- Protects ASF’s reputation for open governance  

[.notes]
--
Give an example: a newcomer reading archives should be able to trace how a decision was made and why.  
Without this, they may feel excluded or distrustful.  
Transparency levels the playing field and makes community growth possible.  
--

== Reflection Question
Your podling makes most decisions on company Slack and only posts summaries to the list afterwards.  
**What specific steps would you take to move decisions back to the list?**  

[.notes]
--
Possible answers:  
- Politely remind team members on Slack that final decisions must be recorded on the list.  
- Summarize Slack discussions yourself and ask others to confirm.  
- Encourage people to “take it to the list” when conversations turn into decisions.  
- If issues persist, mentors can escalate gently by reminding the community of ASF policy.  
--

== 🗳️ Consensus Decision-Making
*How Apache Projects Decide Together*

[.notes]
--
Explain that Apache decisions are made by consensus, not by fiat or corporate mandate.  
Consensus ensures buy-in, avoids surprises, and keeps governance collective.  
Contrast with corporate cultures where decisions might be dictated top-down.  
--

== What Consensus Means
- Everyone on the mailing list can contribute to discussions  
- Objections must be heard and addressed — not ignored  
- “Lazy consensus”: silence is treated as agreement unless someone objects  
- Votes are public, transparent, and archived  

[.notes]
--
Introduce lazy consensus: a default that keeps things moving unless someone objects.  
Explain that explicit votes are used for key decisions like releases or adding committers.  
Stress that objections are valuable signals — they highlight risks or perspectives that need attention.  
--

== Why Consensus Matters
- Ensures decisions reflect the whole community, not just one group  
- Builds durable buy-in and trust  
- Reduces surprises and prevents domination by any vendor or individual  
- Protects ASF’s reputation for neutral, open governance  

[.notes]
--
Use an example: if only one company pushes changes without consensus, outsiders will hesitate to contribute.  
Consensus is what makes a project feel like a community effort rather than corporate property.  
--

== Reflection Question
A decision was announced as “already made” without mailing list discussion.  
**How would you move the decision back to the list and involve the community?**  

[.notes]
--
Possible responses:  
- Ask for the decision to be restated on the list for confirmation.  
- Invite feedback and objections openly.  
- Remind participants that off-list decisions are not valid in ASF governance.  
--

== 🌟 Meritocracy & Community Growth
*Earning Trust Through Contribution*

[.notes]
--
Explain that meritocracy at Apache is about earned trust through contributions, not about status, company, or seniority.  
It’s how newcomers can grow into leaders over time.  
--

== What Meritocracy Means
- Contributors gain responsibility by demonstrating commitment  
- Merit is judged by community, not employers  
- Roles: contributor → committer → PMC/PPMC  
- Contributions can be code, docs, reviews, community work  

== Meritocracy Myths
- ❌ “Meritocracy means the loudest voice wins”  
- ❌ “Only code counts”  
- ❌ “Seniority at a company equals merit at Apache”  
- ✅ Reality: Merit is earned through consistent, constructive contributions across many forms  

[.notes]
--
Clear up common misconceptions: being loud or senior doesn’t equal merit.  
Meritocracy at Apache is practical, earned, and reviewed by peers.  
--

== Why Meritocracy Matters
- Encourages open participation — anyone can rise through contribution  
- Builds a sustainable pipeline of new leaders  
- Prevents concentration of control in a small group or one company  
- Demonstrates ASF’s fairness and openness to the wider community  

[.notes]
--
Link to sustainability: without new committers and PMC members, projects stagnate.  
Meritocracy creates a natural pipeline of leadership.  
--

== Reflection Question
Most contributors in your podling come from one company.  
**What concrete actions could you take to attract and support new contributors from outside that company?**  

[.notes]
--
Possible actions:  
- Improve documentation and onboarding.  
- Label “good first issues.”  
- Mentor newcomers explicitly.  
- Encourage non-company committers to join decision-making.  
--

== 🤝 Community over Code
*Why People Matter Most*

[.notes]
--
This is a core ASF motto: “Community over Code.”  
Even perfect code won’t save a project if the community is weak.  
Strong communities can always improve code.  
--

== What It Means
- Strong communities can always fix weak code  
- Weak communities cannot sustain even great code  
- Decision-making, mentoring, and inclusivity are as important as technical output  
- Graduation depends on community health, not just releases  

== Why It Matters
- Ensures projects outlast individual contributors or companies  
- Encourages collaboration and mentorship  
- Builds resilience against burnout or “bus factor” risks  
- Reinforces ASF’s mission of sustainable, diverse communities  

[.notes]
--
Use examples: projects with 1–2 dominant developers often fail when those developers leave.  
Healthy communities spread knowledge, reduce risk, and create continuity.  
--

== Reflection Question
Your podling ships features quickly but most discussions come from just 2–3 people.  
**What risks do you see, and what actions could you take to spread participation?**  

[.notes]
--
Possible answers:  
- Risk of burnout, low diversity, and project collapse.  
- Actions: encourage more list discussion, assign reviews, mentor new contributors.  
--

== 🏢 Vendor Neutrality
*Balancing Company Involvement*

[.notes]
--
Vendor neutrality is crucial: Apache projects must not be dominated by any single company.  
Individuals contribute in their own name, not on behalf of their employer.  
--

== What It Means
- No single company controls the project or its direction  
- Contributors act as individuals, not company representatives  
- Technical merit matters more than employer or sponsorship  
- Governance is balanced across diverse participants  

== Why It Matters
- Protects ASF’s reputation for independence and fairness  
- Encourages outside contributors to join without fear of corporate control  
- Reduces legal and trademark risks tied to company ownership  
- Builds long-term sustainability as companies come and go  

[.notes]
--
Example: if a podling is 90% run by one vendor, outsiders will see it as that company’s project, not Apache’s.  
This undermines recruitment and credibility.  
--

== Reflection Question
Most code in your podling comes from one company’s employees.  
**What steps could you take to balance this and make the project more welcoming to outsiders?**  

[.notes]
--
Actions could include:  
- Recognizing and mentoring external contributors.  
- Rotating release managers.  
- Encouraging diverse representation in PPMC.  
--

== ™️ Branding & Trademarks
*Protecting the Project’s Identity*

[.notes]
--
Branding may feel like a legal detail, but it’s vital.  
Apache owns names and logos so projects remain community property, not company assets.  
--

== What It Means
- The ASF owns project names and logos  
- Podlings must follow ASF branding guidelines  
- Names, marks, and domains cannot be controlled by individuals or companies  
- Projects must consistently use the **“Apache <Project>”** name in all materials  
- Consistent use of ASF trademarks protects both the project and the Foundation  

== Why It Matters
- Safeguards ASF’s credibility and independence  
- Ensures users know they are getting the official ASF project  
- Prevents legal disputes and confusion  
- Reinforces the project’s status as a community-driven effort  

[.notes]
--
Example: a podling using a company domain or logo confuses users about ownership.  
ASF branding protects both the project and its contributors.  
--

== Reflection Question
A major contributing company is using your podling’s name and logo in its marketing in a way that suggests it “owns” the project.  
**How should your podling respond to protect ASF branding while maintaining a good relationship with that contributor?**  

[.notes]
--
Tricky because the company is a major contributor.  
Podling should:  
- Thank them for their contributions.  
- Remind the list that ASF owns names/logos and branding must not imply company ownership.  
- Suggest compliant wording (e.g., “Company X contributes to Apache <Project>”).  
- Keep the discussion on-list so all see the rule.  
- Escalate to trademarks@apache.org if needed.  

Balance diplomacy with protecting ASF’s independence.  
--
